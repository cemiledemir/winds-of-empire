Winds of Empire — 100 Years at Sea

How four European empires competed for control of the world's oceans, told through 250,000 ship logbook entries

DTU 02806 Social Data Analysis & Visualization — Spring 2026

Between 1750 and 1850, four European nations, Britain, the Netherlands, Spain, and France, sent thousands of ships across every ocean on Earth. Their officers kept daily logbooks, recording wind direction, weather, and their coordinates. These logbooks were originally digitised by climate scientists hoping to reconstruct pre-industrial weather patterns. But in that process, something else surfaced: a century of geopolitical history, written one observation at a time by the men who lived it.

Each record in the dataset represents a single day aboard a single ship. When you count those records by nation and year, the numbers trace the shape of empire itself. You can see which nations were expanding, which were collapsing, and roughly when the balance of power shifted. This page walks through what we found in the data, how it confirms what historians have long argued, and where it challenges assumptions you might bring to the table.

About the data: The CLIWOC 2.1 dataset (Climatological Database for the World's Oceans) contains 287,000+ daily logbook observations from Dutch, British, Spanish, and French ships between 1662 and 1855. After filtering to the period with the richest coverage (1750 to 1850) and cleaning for coordinate validity, we work with 251,456 records. Each record represents one day of one ship's voyage, with position coordinates, wind measurements, and in some cases, the nationality of other ships encountered at sea.

Act 1: Rise & Fall of Empires

The simplest question you can ask of this data is also the most revealing: how many ships did each nation have at sea in a given year? The stacked area chart below plots logbook entries per year for all four empires. Since each entry corresponds to one day on one ship, the count serves as a rough but honest proxy for naval and commercial presence.

Start with Spain. The Spanish line climbs through the 1760s and 1770s, peaking at 5,662 entries in 1778. That number is not a coincidence. The late 1770s were the peak years of the Atlantic silver trade, and Spain's economy ran on the flota system: twice-yearly convoy fleets carrying silver from Vera Cruz and Portobelo back to Cadiz. In 1779, Spain entered the American Revolutionary War on the side of France, and within a year the logbook count dropped from 5,662 to under 800. It never recovered. By 1800, Spain logged just 6 entries in the entire year. The loss of its Atlantic fleet was not gradual. It was sudden and total, and the data captures the exact year it happened.

The Dutch tell a different story, but equally precise. The Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie (the VOC, or Dutch East India Company) had dominated trade between Europe and Southeast Asia for nearly two centuries. In the data, Dutch logbook entries hold steady through the 1780s and early 1790s. Then, in 1795, French revolutionary forces occupied the Netherlands. The VOC was formally dissolved in 1799, and the data reflects this with clinical accuracy: entries fall from 1,292 in 1796 to just 3 in 1800. For the next decade, the Dutch are nearly invisible. But the story does not end there. In the 1820s, the Netherlands Trading Society (Nederlandsche Handel-Maatschappij) began rebuilding colonial trade routes to the East Indies, and Dutch logbook counts climbed back rapidly. By the 1840s, Dutch entries actually exceeded British ones, a fact that would surprise anyone who thinks of this century as belonging solely to Britain.

France is where the data corrects a common assumption. You might expect the Louisiana Purchase of 1803 to mark France's retreat from the seas, as Napoleon sold off a vast colonial territory and redirected resources toward continental war. But the data tells us the collapse began fourteen years earlier. In 1788, French logbooks recorded 884 entries. In 1789, the year the Revolution broke out, that number fell to 48. That is a 95% drop in a single year. The Revolution did not just change France's government; it dismantled the institutional structure that organized naval expeditions. Officers were purged, ports disrupted, and the chain of command destroyed. By the time Napoleon sold Louisiana in 1803, French ships had already been absent from the logbook record for over a decade. The sale of Louisiana was a consequence of France's naval collapse, not the cause of it.

Britain stands apart from all three. British logbook entries remain consistently between 12,000 and 16,000 per decade across the entire century. Wars came and went. The American colonies declared independence. Napoleon rose and fell. Through all of it, British ships kept sailing and kept writing. The reason is structural: the British East India Company, chartered in 1600, held a monopoly on Indian Ocean and East Asian trade for most of this period, and the Royal Navy maintained a global patrol mandate that no other power could match. The data does not show British dominance as a dramatic event. It shows it as a baseline, the one constant in a century of upheaval.

Act 2: Highways of the Sea

The first act told us how many ships each empire had at sea. The natural follow-up is where they went. The map below plots sampled voyage segments for all four nations, colour-coded by empire. When you look at the routes side by side, what stands out is not just the scale of movement, but how differently each empire organized its maritime trade.

The Spanish routes cluster heavily in the Caribbean Sea and the Gulf of Mexico. This is the geographic footprint of the treasure fleet system. Twice a year, convoys of galleons left Vera Cruz and Portobelo loaded with silver and colonial goods, sailing together across the Atlantic to Cadiz for protection against piracy and rival navies. You can also see a thinner Pacific corridor: the Manila Galleon route, linking Acapulco to the Philippines. What is absent is equally telling. Spain has virtually no presence in the Indian Ocean. Its empire was an Atlantic and Pacific affair, and the data makes that geographic limitation visible.

The Dutch pattern is perhaps the most distinctive of the four. Nearly all Dutch routes follow a single corridor: departing from Texel in the North Sea, sailing south along the West African coast, rounding the Cape of Good Hope, and continuing east to Batavia (modern-day Jakarta). This is the VOC highway. The Dutch did not pursue global coverage. They built one highly optimized trade route and ran it with industrial efficiency. The narrowness of the Dutch network in the data confirms what historians have written about the VOC's model: it prioritized throughput on a fixed corridor over territorial breadth.

French routes appear thin and scattered by comparison. The heaviest concentration is in the Caribbean, connecting ports in Saint-Domingue (modern Haiti) and Martinique with Atlantic crossing routes. You can also see a West African leg, consistent with French involvement in the Atlantic slave trade, and some routes to Ile de France (Mauritius) in the Indian Ocean. The overall impression is of a nation spread across too many theaters without the fleet size to dominate any of them, which is consistent with France's pattern of losing colonial wars to Britain throughout this period.

Britain is the exception to every pattern. British routes cover all three major oceans, extending from the Arctic down to latitude 71 degrees south. The densest corridor runs eastward through the Indian Ocean, reflecting the East India Company's trade monopoly with India, Ceylon, and China. But there are also routes in the Caribbean, along the West African coast, across the North Atlantic to the American colonies, and deep into the Pacific. No other nation in the dataset comes close to this geographic range, and the data confirms what the entry counts in Act 1 already suggested: Britain's advantage was not just in fleet size, but in the sheer breadth of where its ships operated.

A note on the triangle trade
The route map shows West African coastal legs for several nations, consistent with the transatlantic slave trade. However, the CLIWOC dataset was built for meteorological research, not cargo tracking. Only 39 records in the entire dataset contain references to enslaved people in the cargo notes, all of them Dutch, dating between 1751 and 1789. This almost certainly represents a tiny fraction of actual slave-trade voyages. The routes are visible; the human cost behind them is not captured here.

Act 3: Encounter Hotspots

The first two acts looked at each empire individually: how many ships they had and where those ships went. But ships did not sail in isolation. Officers frequently recorded sightings of other vessels, sometimes noting the nationality of the ship they passed. The CLIWOC dataset includes over 29,000 of these encounter records, and they open up a different kind of question: not just where empires were, but where they met.

The most immediate finding is that self-encounters dominate the record. British ships recorded 26,584 sightings of other British ships, compared to 354 sightings of Dutch ships, 166 of Spanish ships, and 130 of French ships. The Dutch recorded 1,055 sightings of other Dutch ships. These numbers reflect something practical: nations concentrated their shipping on specific routes, so ships of the same flag were far more likely to encounter each other than ships of a rival power. The British self-encounter count is so high partly because British ships were simply everywhere, making it statistically likely that any vessel you passed was also flying a British flag.

The cross-national encounters are smaller in number but historically richer. The strongest cross-national link is British-Dutch, at 354 recorded encounters. This makes geographic sense: both nations operated heavily in the North Sea and both maintained Indian Ocean trade routes through the Cape of Good Hope. The British-Spanish count of 166 reflects overlapping Caribbean and Atlantic operations. British-French encounters, at 130, were concentrated in similar waters.

What makes the encounter data genuinely interesting to explore is the question of correlation with diplomatic status. The dense dot clusters in the encounter map below — the English Channel, the Caribbean, the Cape of Good Hope, and the waters around Java — are precisely the locations where major naval battles took place during this century. This raises a compelling question: do the encounter counts change during wartime versus peacetime? If they do, it could indicate that wartime patrols increased the likelihood of contact. If they do not, it would suggest that these were structurally inevitable collision points, places where imperial trade routes crossed regardless of whether the empires were at war or at peace. The dataset does not include a war/peace indicator per encounter, so this remains an open question, but the geographic overlap between encounter hotspots and known battle sites is hard to ignore.

An unexpected signal
The encounter data includes 71 recorded sightings of American ships by Dutch vessels. Since the United States only began operating a significant merchant fleet after 1780, these encounters serve as a quiet timestamp: the emergence of a new maritime nation, visible in a European meteorological archive.

What This Data Can and Cannot Tell You

This dataset was never meant to tell a political story. It was assembled by climate scientists to reconstruct wind patterns in the pre-industrial Atlantic and Indian Oceans. The fact that it also encodes geopolitical history is a byproduct of how the data was produced: empires with more ships generated more logbook entries, and the geographic distribution of those entries reflects where each empire directed its commercial and military attention.

There is real power in that kind of incidental evidence. The CLIWOC data confirms, with specific years and entry counts, events that traditional historical sources describe in broader strokes: Spain's Atlantic collapse after 1779, the institutional death of the VOC in 1799, the destruction of French naval capacity during the Revolution, and the quiet persistence of British global reach. These are not interpretations layered on top of the data. They are visible in the most basic summary statistics.

But it is equally important to be clear about what the data cannot support. Coverage is uneven across nations: British and Dutch records are far more numerous than Spanish and French ones, partly because of archival survival and partly because of how the CLIWOC project selected logbooks for digitisation. Encounter records exist for only about 12% of all entries, and we cannot distinguish between a peaceful commercial sighting and a hostile wartime encounter from the data alone. The dataset contains almost no information about cargo, crew, or the human consequences of these voyages. The triangle trade is visible in the route patterns, but the dataset was not designed to document it, and treating absence of evidence as evidence of absence would be a mistake.

We structured this page as a magazine-style narrative following the framework described by Segel and Heer (2010): a linear, author-driven sequence of three acts, each built around a visualization that answers a specific question. The first act asks how many, the second asks where, and the third asks who met whom. This format works well for presenting a predetermined argument while still allowing you to explore the interactive visualizations at your own pace.

If the analysis here interests you, the dataset rewards further exploration. Temporal animation of the route data, text analysis of the wind vocabulary used by each nation, network graphs of encounter relationships, and cross-referencing with external datasets on naval battles or trade volumes are all viable next steps. The CLIWOC archive is one of the richest quantitative records of the age of sail, and we have only scratched its surface here.
